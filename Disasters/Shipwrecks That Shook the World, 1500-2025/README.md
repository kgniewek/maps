From the early sixteenth century onward, the opening of regular ocean routes between Europe, Africa, the Americas and Asia put ever larger ships, richer cargoes and growing numbers of passengers at the mercy of the sea. Portuguese carracks rounding southern Africa, Spanish treasure fleets crossing the Caribbean and Dutch and English East Indiamen sailing to the Indian Ocean all lost vessels to storms, reefs and faulty navigation. Many of these early losses carried silver, spices or colonists, and their wrecks shaped imperial rivalries, the fortunes of trading companies and the course of settlement in places as distant as Florida, Bermuda and Western Australia.

Warfare added a second great cause of loss. The Spanish Armada of 1588 was wrecked as much by Atlantic gales along the Irish and Scottish coasts as by English guns, while later naval conflicts, from the Battle of the Nile to Tsushima, Jutland and the Pacific campaigns of the Second World War, destroyed whole fleets in single days. Passenger liners such as the Lusitania and the Wilhelm Gustloff became casualties of submarine campaigns, and the sinking of civilian ships repeatedly changed public opinion and the conduct of wars.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought steam, iron hulls and mass migration, and with them disasters of unprecedented scale. Emigrant ships such as the Tayleur and the Cataraqui, excursion steamers such as the General Slocum and the Princess Alice, and liners such as the Titanic and the Empress of Ireland went down within sight of land or in busy shipping lanes. Collisions, groundings in fog, boiler explosions and fires revealed how far technology had outrun safety rules, lifeboat provision and wireless communication. Public inquiries after these events led to landmark reforms, including international conventions on lifeboat capacity, radio watches, ice patrols and the construction of hulls.

In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries the largest losses of life shifted to overcrowded ferries in the developing world, while oil tankers such as the Torrey Canyon, the Amoco Cadiz and the Exxon Valdez made the environmental cost of shipping a matter of global concern. Modern cruise ships, car carriers and superyachts have shown that size and sophistication do not remove the hazards of weather, human error and design flaws. Because many wrecks lie in deep or remote water, and because accounts of older disasters are fragmentary, exact dates, positions and casualty figures from many periods remain disputed or approximate.
